01Starting PN — first 72 hours

Extremely low birth weight (ELBW) infants have only ~200 kcal of glucose stores at birth and lose 1–2% of body protein per day on glucose alone, versus a fetus receiving ~4 g/kg/day of protein in utero. Early amino acids are protective, not risky.

02Calcium : phosphorus ratio

First 4–7 postnatal days: a lower molar ratio of 0.8–1:1 (Ca:P) reduces hypophosphatemia and increases eucalcemia. After the first days, once amino acids/energy are optimized, 1–1.3:1 gives the best bone mineralization supply. Ca and Pi can be increased until both are excreted simultaneously at low concentration (>1.0 mmol/L) in urine.

03The evidence caution

The NEON trial found no benefit of immediate full-dose amino acids over incremental introduction on lean body mass or liver fat — and more metabolic disturbance with immediate dosing. PEPaNIC (delayed PN in critically ill children) showed fewer infections with delayed PN, but did not include preterm infants and shouldn't be directly extrapolated to them.

04Monitoring on PN

  • Blood glucose — titrate glucose infusion rate
  • Blood urea — titrate amino acid intake
  • Triglycerides — titrate lipid intake, hold/reduce if >265 mg/dL
  • Plasma & urinary electrolytes routinely, to guide Na/K provision

01Starting minimal enteral / trophic feeds

ESPGHAN 2022 (strong recommendation): minimal enteral feeding should begin as soon as possible after birth, preferably within 24 hours (and always within 48 h). A 24–48 hour wait for mother's own milk before using formula is reasonable. Trophic feeds of 10–25 mL/kg/day show a trend to less feeding intolerance and faster achievement of full feeds, with no increase in NEC. Contraindication: intestinal obstruction or ileus.

02Advancement rates

Birth weightInitial rateNotes
<1,000 g15–20 mL/kg/dConsider increasing after 2–3 days if tolerated
≥1,000 g30–35 mL/kg/dFaster increases (up to ~30–35) do not raise NEC risk

The SIFT trial (Cochrane) found no difference in NEC between slow and fast advancement (RR 1.07, 95% CI 0.83–1.39). Full enteral feeding is usually 150–160 mL/kg/day; up to 180–200 mL/kg/day is tolerated by many HM-fed infants.

03Continuous vs. bolus, PN taper

No consistent difference in days to full feeds, growth, or NEC between continuous and bolus/intermittent feeding — choose what the infant tolerates. Because 40–50% of enteral amino acids are used in first-pass gut/liver metabolism (up to 70% for threonine), don't taper PN amino acids before enteral intake reaches at least 75 mL/kg/day, and don't stop PN before full enteral feeding.

04Standardizable protocol elements

  • What to feed: mother's milk → donor milk → preterm formula
  • Timing of initiation (early, within 24–48h)
  • Duration of trophic feeding (≤3 days shortens time to full feeds without more NEC)
  • Rate of advancement
  • Continuous vs. bolus
  • Whether/when to check gastric residuals

01Who and when

Fortify human milk for all very preterm infants and those <1,800 g. Unfortified HM cannot supply adequate protein/mineral intake even at very high, poorly-tolerated volumes — at 250–300 mL/kg/day of unfortified preterm HM, calcium and phosphorus intake still only reaches 50–60% of the lower recommended limit.

Start full-strength multicomponent fortifier (MCF) at 50–100 mL/kg/day of enteral feeds. There's no evidence that starting at half-strength or waiting longer is safer — it only delays adequate nutrient intake. Six studies found no increase in feeding intolerance or NEC with "early" fortification (started at 20–60 mL/kg/day).

02Which fortifier

Bovine milk-based MCF is the current standard of care. Cochrane evidence shows no demonstrated benefit of human milk-derived over bovine milk-based fortifiers for NEC, mortality, feeding intolerance, infection, or growth (low certainty, 1 study). Newer higher-protein bovine MCFs achieve weight gain of 18–20 g/kg/day during fortification, closer to fetal growth rates.

02Absorption & monitoring

  • Enteral calcium absorption: ~50–65% of intake
  • Enteral phosphorus absorption: ~90%, more consistent
  • Magnesium absorption: ~40–50% (enteral target set 40% above accretion)
  • Check magnesium once in week 1 (day 3–5) on PN
  • Vitamin D deficiency threshold: <20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L); consider >30 ng/mL sufficient
  • Consider checking Ca/P/25(OH)D together at 4–6 weeks on full enteral feeds

03Practical PN tip

Ca:P ratio of 0.8–1:1 in the first 4–7 days reduces hypophosphatemia risk; 1–1.3:1 thereafter supports bone mineral accretion. Solubility limits (precipitation risk) constrain how much Ca/P can be delivered parenterally — consider calcium glycerophosphate or cysteine addition where available.

Not routinely added to PN (overload risk, no approved IV product). Follow ferritin serially; if <35–70 µg/L, increase dose up to 3–4 (max 6) mg/kg/day for a limited period. Infants on erythropoietin need up to 6 mg/kg/day. Iron is not risk-free: excess is linked to diarrhea, infection risk, and impaired growth/neurodevelopment.

02Zinc & selenium

Zinc: enteral 2.0–2.25 mg/kg/day (up to 3 mg/kg/day if <1,500 g). An RCT of high-dose zinc (6.6 vs 0.9 mg/kg/day) found lower morbidity, less NEC (0% vs 6.3%), lower mortality — but that dose exceeds standard nutritional requirement, so distinguish nutritional replacement from pharmacologic dosing.

Selenium: enteral 7–10 µg/kg/day; parenteral 7 µg/kg/day. Improves selenium status and may reduce sepsis risk in VLBW infants; no adverse effects seen at these doses.

03Iodine & manganese

Iodine: enteral 10–55 µg/kg/day; parenteral 1–10 µg/kg/day (favor 10 µg/kg in long-term PN — 3 µg/kg/day has been linked to negative iodine balance). Fetal deficiency causes irreversible brain injury; if suspected, check thyroid status.

Manganese: deficiency is rarely reported; the bigger clinical concern is excess accumulation, so avoid over-supplementation, especially in cholestasis.

04Vitamins E and K

Vitamin E: enteral 2.2–11 mg/kg/day (human milk alone provides far too little — fortification/preterm formula needed). Deficiency (~6–8 weeks of age) can cause hemolytic anemia, thrombocytosis, edema. Keep serum α-tocopherol below ~35 mg/L — higher levels are linked to sepsis and NEC risk.

Vitamin K: placental transfer is minimal (cord levels often undetectable), so standard IM prophylaxis at birth remains essential; vitamin K3 (synthetic) is avoided due to hemolysis risk in G6PD-deficient infants.

01Gastric residuals — the evidence has shifted

Routine gastric residual checks are no longer recommended as standard practice. RCT evidence shows infants who skip routine pre-feed residual checks reach full enteral feeding faster, with equivalent (not worse) rates of NEC, sepsis, and ventilator-associated pneumonia. Discarding residuals — including bile-tinged ones — removes physiologically useful bile acids and gut hormones without solid evidence of benefit.

Reserve residual checks for infants who already show other signs of intolerance — don't use volume alone to hold feeds.

02Signs that do matter

  • Abdominal distension with tenderness or erythema
  • Bilious or blood-stained vomiting
  • Gross or occult blood in stool
  • Decreased bowel sounds
  • New apnea, bradycardia, temperature instability, poor perfusion

Abdominal girth alone is a poor predictor (varies up to 3.5 cm in normal feeding cycles) — use it alongside exam findings, not in isolation.

03Reflux management — what not to do

  • Prokinetics (domperidone, metoclopramide) — not recommended; QTc risk, may worsen reflux
  • Acid suppressants (ranitidine, omeprazole) — linked to more NEC and late-onset sepsis
  • Feed thickeners — no neonatal RCTs; some agents linked to NEC/lactobezoar

Positioning: left lateral immediately post-feed, then prone (with 30° head elevation) after ~30 minutes shows lowest reflux by pH-MII monitoring.

04Recognizing NEC

Presentation ranges from subtle (increasing apnea/bradycardia, feeding intolerance evolving over days) to sudden shock and multi-organ dysfunction. Advancing disease brings abdominal distension, tenseness, and periumbilical erythema. No lab test is diagnostic — CBC and inflammatory markers have poor specificity; pneumatosis intestinalis or portal venous gas on imaging is the clearer signal. Management centers on stopping feeds, gastric decompression, IV antibiotics (typically 7–10 days), and PN support while the bowel heals; surgery/drainage follows perforation.

02Which bag, when

  • Starter PN — birth to 24h of life, before first electrolyte results are back. Contains no potassium or chloride, avoiding early hyperkalemia risk in an oliguric first day.
  • Standard Preterm PN — default maintenance bag once Starter PN is stepped down, for preterm infants with normal electrolytes.
  • High Sodium Preterm PN — hyponatraemic preterm infants needing extra sodium without simply increasing total fluid volume.
  • 7.5% Dextrose Preterm PN — hyperglycaemic preterm infants; lower dextrose load (75 g/L vs 100 g/L) reduces glucose infusion rate at the same volume.
  • Standard Term PN — term infants following an initial starter phase; lower amino acid/mineral density reflecting lower term requirements.
  • Peripheral Preterm/Term PN — for infants without central access; calcium and phosphorus are deliberately capped (Ca 3.5 mmol/L, P 3 mmol/L) to keep the solution within peripheral-line solubility and phlebitis limits.
  • Fluid Restricted Preterm PN — for infants needing fluid restriction (e.g., significant PDA, evolving BPD, cardiac failure) while still meeting nutrient targets at a lower volume.

03Practical notes

Both peripheral formulations run at a similar osmolarity to the central bags (902–931 mOsm/L) — the calcium/phosphorus restriction protects the vein from precipitation and infiltration injury, not from osmolarity alone. Follow your unit's peripheral line dwell-time and site-monitoring policy regardless.

Cross-check any dose outside these standard bags — or any patient who can't be met nutritionally by them — against an individualized prescription, using the Pharmacist's Handbook of Parenteral Nutrition in Neonates and Paediatrics, 2nd Ed. (2023) and the general ESPGHAN/ESPEN/ESPR/CSPEN targets in the Parenteral Nutrition tab as your reference ranges.

01What it is, and who's at risk

Neonatal refeeding-like syndrome mirrors the adult/paediatric picture: a cluster of fluid and electrolyte disturbances — hypophosphatemia (the hallmark), often with hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hyperglycemia, and sodium/fluid imbalance — triggered when parenteral nutrition delivers amino acids and energy faster than a nutritionally depleted infant can safely handle. Risk is highest in intrauterine growth-restricted (IUGR) preterm infants, particularly those born after placental insufficiency, who start with inadequate mineral stores. Fatal outcomes haven't been reported in the VLBW population specifically, but the metabolic disturbance itself is common and preventable with planned monitoring.

02Monitoring in at-risk infants

  • Baseline electrolytes, magnesium, and phosphate (plus fluid balance) around 24h of life — ideally before the next PN prescription is written
  • Trend Ca:P ratio on PN (this unit runs ~1–1.3:1 after the first days; see Parenteral Nutrition tab)
  • Be cautious advancing amino acids further when phosphate is trending down without a matched increase in phosphate delivery that same day

04Hypophosphatemia — stepwise management

Mild: restrict PN amino acids to 3 g/kg/day; recheck full electrolytes/Mg/phosphate before any further advance; adjust Ca:P ratio (e.g. toward 2:1 if on central access and PN <100 mL/kg/day, shifting toward 1:1 if serum calcium is also elevated); enteral sodium phosphate 0.5 mmol/kg/day, titrating up to 2 mmol/kg/day.

Moderate: same PN adjustments, plus consider IV sodium or potassium phosphate if there's clinical concern for evolving refeeding-like syndrome and phosphate is trending down on consecutive checks.

Severe: sodium phosphate 0.25 mmol/kg IV once, or potassium phosphate 0.18 mmol/kg IV once. Potassium phosphate contains aluminium — reserve for when sodium phosphate is undesirable on the labs, and use discretion with potassium in renal impairment.

05Hypokalemia & hypomagnesemia

Hypokalemia, mild: adjust PN potassium as tolerated; enteral potassium chloride 0.5–1 mEq/kg/day (may increase as tolerated).

Hypokalemia, moderate/severe: IV potassium chloride 0.5–1 mEq/kg once (use discretion in renal impairment).

Hypomagnesemia: IV magnesium sulfate 0.4 mEq/kg once; no standard enteral repletion.

These stepwise doses are adapted from a published NICU electrolyte-repletion protocol as a practical framework — confirm against your own unit's refeeding/electrolyte guideline before prescribing.

01Who to screen

  • Gestational age <30 weeks or birth weight <1,250 g
  • Total parenteral nutrition >4 weeks
  • Unable to reach full fortified enteral feeds
  • Prolonged exposure to drugs that impair bone mineralization (diuretics, corticosteroids, methylxanthines)

02Screening & trigger for workup

Check serum calcium, phosphate, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) at day 28 of life in at-risk infants. Proceed to full workup if phosphate <1.8 mmol/L, or ALP >800 IU/L, or ALP >500 IU/L and trending upward.

For context: ALP >900 IU/L with phosphate <1.8 mmol/L at 3 weeks of age predicts bone mineral deficiency at 3 months with high sensitivity; a peak ALP around 650 IU/L at 4–5 postnatal weeks is similarly predictive of low bone mineral density at term.

03Workup & treatment algorithm

On trigger: send serum intact PTH (iPTH) and 25(OH) vitamin D. If phosphate <1.8 mmol/L, also send urine phosphate, urine calcium, and urine creatinine to calculate tubular reabsorption of phosphate (TRP).

In both pathways: ensure the infant is on fortified breast milk or preterm formula, plus an oral multivitamin (e.g., Appeton Baby Drops — see Local Supplements tab). Start vitamin D if 25(OH)D <50 nmol/L; if <25 nmol/L, repeat the level after 8 weeks of treatment.

04Monitoring & stopping criteria

  • Repeat Ca/phosphate/ALP every 1–2 weeks
  • Repeat iPTH at 4 and 8 weeks of treatment, then individualize
  • Adjust supplement doses every 1–2 weeks based on biochemical response
  • Reduce the calcium dose if serum calcium rises above the normal range
  • Stop phosphate once phosphate and ALP normalize
  • Stop calcium and vitamin D once serum iPTH normalizes

05Tubular reabsorption of phosphate (TRP)

TRP (%) = [1 − (urine P/urine creatinine × serum creatinine/serum P)] × 100

Normal TRP: 78–91%. TRP >85% is a marker of insufficient phosphate supplementation — a nudge toward increasing phosphate rather than assuming the kidney is simply conserving it appropriately.

This algorithm reflects one Malaysian neonatal unit's local protocol (adapted from Chinoy et al., Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed 2019, and Rayannavar & Calabria, Semin Fetal Neonatal Med 2020). Check it against your own unit's MBD guideline — thresholds here are deliberately more conservative-to-act than the general enteral/parenteral mineral targets in the Minerals tab.
